# Methodology

How every number in the week 3 post (the Instagram slides and the page at https://mediancanadian.ca/bc-election-oct26/) was produced, so anyone can check or challenge it.

## The question

British Columbia voted on October 24, 2026, in a snap election called on September 22, 2026. In Elections BC's results as of October 25, 2026, 1:49 a.m. Pacific, the Conservative Party of BC has a majority: 64 of 93 seats, its first majority since 1928, after nine years of NDP government. The post asks how big the change was against 2024, how a party with 2% of the vote in 2020 got there, where the vote moved, which kinds of places moved most, how the areas that voted Liberal in the 2025 federal election voted provincially, what voters said before the vote, and what the Conservatives promised on each top worry.

All 2026 numbers are Elections BC's results as of October 25, 2026, 1:49 a.m. Pacific (7,237 of 7,268 ballot boxes counted). The final count, which adds mail-in, out-of-district and other ballots, is planned for November 6 to 10, 2026. In 2024, three of the four kinds of ballots left to the 2026 final count (vote-by-mail, assisted telephone and absentee ballots on Final Voting Day) leaned NDP, so the narrowest Conservative margins are the most likely to change.

## Data and method, by chapter

1. **The result (cover and slide 2).** Seats and vote shares for 2024 from Elections BC's Statement of Votes, and for 2026 from Elections BC's official results files as posted October 25, 2026, 1:49 a.m. Pacific. A seat goes to the candidate with the most votes in each riding; 47 of 93 seats make a majority, and seats to spare are the seats above 47. The margin is the first party's share of the vote minus the second party's. "First majority since 1928" compares 2026 with the seats each party held after every general election since 1928 (Electoral History of British Columbia 1871 to 1986, and the Statements of Votes); the 1945 and 1949 majorities belonged to the Liberal-Conservative Coalition, in which the Conservatives were the junior partner. The website adds a swingometer (a uniform shift of each riding's Conservative and NDP vote, a simplification), the tipping point, and the Gallagher least-squares index of disproportionality, the Laakso-Taagepera effective number of parties and the Pedersen volatility index for 1928 to 2026, computed from party-family shares and seats.
2. **How they got here (slide 3).** Elections BC Statements of Votes and the Electoral History of British Columbia for 1928 to 2024, and the 2026 results above. The 2001 Conservative share comes from Elections BC's 2001 results by district, since the series counts it under Other. "Took the BC Liberals' place as the NDP's main rival" refers to the party that finished second in share of the vote (BC Liberal in 2020, Conservative in 2024); it says nothing about how individual voters moved. The 2020 results were moved onto the 2023 ridings by voting area, weighted by 2024 registered voters, so 2020 can be compared riding by riding on the website.
3. **Where it changed (slide 4).** The party elected in each riding in 2026 compared with the 2024 certified winner. Vancouver's suburbs are the Metro Vancouver ridings outside the City of Vancouver (names that do not start with "Vancouver-"). The Metro Vancouver map uses the full-resolution boundaries (lightly smoothed, to about 12 metres), with the sea, lakes over 1 km² and rivers over 50 hectares from the BC Freshwater Atlas drawn as water; its south edge is the Canada-United States border. On the website, swing is the change in the Conservative share of the Conservative and NDP vote, which reduces the effect of 2024 independents and of Green candidates entering or leaving a riding. Peace River North, with no NDP candidate in 2026, is left out. In the five ridings where the Greens finished first or second, the swing stays in the averages, and each riding's own description gives the race as it was. Place types follow the commuting method of the Canadian Suburbs project (David Gordon, Queen's University), applied to whole ridings with provincial averages, a simplification: exurban and rural below 150 people per square kilometre; then active core where walking and cycling to work is at least 1.5 times the provincial rate; transit suburb where transit use is above the provincial rate; car suburb otherwise. The project applies the walking test first, to census tracts within metropolitan areas; whole ridings are far larger, so the density test comes first here. Regions follow regional districts, with West Vancouver-Sea to Sky and Powell River-Sunshine Coast in "Rest of Vancouver Island and Coast".
4. **By household income (slide 5).** Ridings are ranked by median household income (2020 incomes, 2021 Census) and cut into four groups of 23, with Peace River North (no NDP candidate) left out; each group's swing is the average change in the Conservative share of the Conservative and NDP vote, 2024 to 2026, and each group's income is the median of its ridings' medians. Census figures are the 2021 Census profiles for the 93 ridings published by BC Stats on the 2023 boundaries. The website's profiles of the ridings that switched or stayed sum counts across each group, each over its own census universe: owner and renter households for owners (band housing left out), commuters for travel to work by car as driver or passenger, and population in private households for visible minorities. The ridings that switched are the 21 that changed to the Conservatives; Courtenay-Comox, which changed to the NDP, and the two ridings that elected Greens both years are left out. These describe places and say nothing about how any one person voted.
5. **The middle ground (slide 6).** Elections Canada's 2025 poll-by-poll results were allocated to provincial ridings by the share of each polling division's 2021 population (dissemination blocks) inside each riding. Advance and special ballots, which have no polling division, were spread within each federal riding by its election-day pattern; party totals match Elections Canada's B.C. totals within 5 votes per party. Ridings are grouped by the party with the most federal votes. Because the votes are allocated, the grouping is an estimate, and five ridings are within 0.5 point; the table gives the slide's figures without the closest of them. The slide shows the provincial vote summed over the ridings where the Liberals had the most federal votes (party votes over all valid votes, 2024 final count and 2026 as of October 25, 2026, 1:49 a.m. Pacific), and the share of the Conservatives' net seat change that came in those ridings. The website also shows riding-level estimates (non-negative Goodman regression with bootstrap ranges) of how each federal party's vote divided provincially; they are estimates from riding totals and describe groups of ridings, so individual voters may differ.
6. **Before the vote (slide 7).** Pollster releases: the Angus Reid Institute's top issues (field work October 15 to 19, 2026; B.C. adults; up to three answers each, so the shares add to more than 100%) and Innovative Research Group's party rated best on each issue (October 15 to 19, 2026; 1,153 people surveyed, weighted to 1,000). A party is shown as rated best only when its margin over the other is larger than the 95% margin of error that a probability sample of the same size (1,000) would have on the gap between the two parties' shares; smaller gaps are shown as about even. The pollster notes that a margin of error cannot be calculated for its online panel. The Angus Reid Institute surveyed B.C. adults, so the website says British Columbians. Ipsos's "time for change" question is on the website.
7. **What comes next (slide 8).** The Conservative Party of BC's 2026 platform ("Lorne Doerkson's Plan for a Stronger BC"): one promise for each of the three worries on the slide, and every promise matched to a worry, with its pages, on the website. Elections BC for the final count and Return Day; the party's board statement for the leadership.

News reports were used only to find official documents; no number comes from a news story. The methods borrowed from published research (place types, income groups, disproportionality, volatility, tipping point, ecological regression) are cited on the website.
